People First Language and Identity First Language

The American Psychological Association "Inclusive Language Guide" explains that person-first language was first applied to issues regarding people with disabilities and, since then, the language has been broadened to refer to other identity groups as well. People are encouraged to use terms and descriptions that both honor and explain person-first and identity-first perspectives. Either is appropriate depending on what the person prefers. Person-first and identity-first language are described in the following ways:

In person-first language, the person is emphasized over the disability, status, etc. (e.g., a person with a disability).

In identity-first language, the individual choose(s) their identity rather than permitting others (e.g., authors, educators, researchers) to name it or to select terms with negative implications. Identity-first language is often used as an expression of cultural pride and a reclamation that once conferred a negative identity.

As person-first and identity-first language is guided by the individual and how they view their identity, self-identification is a key concept.

Self-Identification

The Style Guide for Inclusive Language December 2017 states, "Allowing people to self-identify can help validate experiences, cultures, and struggles. In fact, a person's chosen labels can serve as a quick and easy way of explaining their lived experience within a community, and … (people) should aim, whenever possible, to include language which respects and reflects how people choose to talk about themselves" (p.2).

The Centers for Disease Control's Preferred Terms for Select Populations and Communities further states that best practice for identifying language choice includes, "engaging people from the population or community of focus to find out what they prefer." Whenever possible in our data gathering efforts, we provide opportunities for participants to self-identify.

In addition to ensuring that self-identification options are available for our participants, we work closely with clients to determine the proper language choices for the communities served. At times, this can lead to reflective conversations with clients as they may unintentionally use language choices that fall in the "instead of" categories listed below. In these instances, we hold discussions with our clients to better understand their language choices and determine an equitable course of action that upholds OMNI's values, the clients', as well as those of the communities of focus.
