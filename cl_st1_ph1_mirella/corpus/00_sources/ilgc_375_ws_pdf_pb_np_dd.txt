
RECOMMENDED LANGUAGE AND TERMINOLOGY

SAY THIS: Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, First Nations peoples.
INSTEAD OF: Aboriginals, Aborigines, Native Australians. Do not abbreviate 'Aboriginal' or 'Torres Strait Islander' or use the acronym 'ATSI'.
WHY: Recognises and respects the diversity of cultures, languages, kinship structures and ways of life of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples. Using an acronym disrespects different identities and can be associated with former, painful government policies.

SAY THIS: First Peoples/Nations, Country, Land, Elders, Traditional Custodians, Acknowledgement of Country.
INSTEAD OF: first peoples/nations, country, land, elders, traditional custodians, acknowledgement of country (lowercase).
WHY: As capitalisation demonstrates respect, 'Aboriginal' and 'Torres Strait Islander' should always be capitalised as well as 'Elders', 'Traditional Custodians' and other terms. Important cultural places and practices are also capitalised.

SAY THIS: Colonisation.
INSTEAD OF: Settled, discovered. For example, in phrases like: 'Captain Cook discovered Australia' or 'Australia was first settled in 1788'.
WHY: Australia was not settled peacefully, and sovereignty was never ceded. Describing the arrival of the Europeans as a 'discovery' or 'settlement' is a view of Australian history from the perspective of the United Kingdom rather than Australia.

SAY THIS: 'Historical' or 'pre-colonial'; 'Enduring' or 'ongoing'.
INSTEAD OF: Avoid the terms 'lost', 'past', or 'was' when referring to First Nations cultures and Land.
WHY: It ignores the fact that Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander cultures include the oldest continuous living cultures in the world, dated to be over 65,000 years old.

SAY THIS: 'Histories,' 'perspectives,' 'ways of being,' 'contributions'.
INSTEAD OF: 'History,' 'perspective,' 'way of being,' 'contribution'.
WHY: Pluralisation acknowledges that Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples are not homogenous.

SAY OR DO THIS: Colonisation.
INSTEAD OF: Settled, discovered. For example, in phrases like: 'Abel Tasman discovered New Zealand' or 'New Zealand was first settled in the 1830s'.
WHY: Our language should reflect that Maori occupied Aotearoa centuries before the earliest Europeans, following waves of migration from the Pacific. Referring to the arrival of Europeans as 'settlement' is incorrect and disregards Maori's extraordinary navigational skills and migration experiences.

SAY OR DO THIS: Try to use te reo Maori correctly, with respect and humility.
INSTEAD OF: Not making a genuine attempt to use te reo Maori correctly or using it in a cynical manner.
WHY: Shows respect for the language and culture and helps improve your skills over time.

SAY OR DO THIS: Understand the context, meaning, and use of kupu Maori (Maori words) or whakatauki (Maori proverbs) before using them in writing or korero (speech).
INSTEAD OF: Using Maori words or proverbs in a token-like manner without understanding their meaning or context.
WHY: Proper use demonstrates respect and ensures your message is conveyed accurately.

SAY OR DO THIS: Try to pronounce te reo Maori correctly, paying special attention to names and placenames.
INSTEAD OF: Not making a genuine attempt to correctly pronounce te reo Maori.
WHY: Correct pronunciation shows respect and preserves the meaning of words ensuring clear communication.

SAY OR DO THIS: Use tohuto (macrons) or double vowels correctly to indicate long vowel sounds. Download a Maori keyboard to simplify this process.
INSTEAD OF: Neglecting the use of tohuto or double vowels. Using a symbol over a vowel that is not a tohuto. For example: é or ö.
WHY: Correct usage preserves the meaning of words and demonstrates respect for the language.

SAY OR DO THIS: Respect dialectal differences. For example: Waikato-Tainui use double vowels instead of tohuto.
INSTEAD OF: Assuming or ignoring regional variations in te reo Maori.
WHY: Respecting dialectal differences acknowledges and honours the diversity within the language.

SAY OR DO THIS: Omit adding an 's' to kupu Maori when referring to a plural; there is no 's' in te reo Maori. For example: one kiwi, two kiwi.
INSTEAD OF: Treating te reo Maori as an offshoot of English and adding an 's' to kupu Maori.
WHY: Demonstrates respect and helps preserve the language.

SAY OR DO THIS: Use kupu Maori rather than a transliteration whenever possible. For example: Tamaki Makaurau is the correct way to refer to Auckland rather than its transliteration 'Akarana'. This applies to months and days too. However, this does not apply to people's given names.
INSTEAD OF: Using old-style transliteration of European words in te reo Maori.
WHY: Demonstrates respect and helps preserve the language.

SAY THIS: Older person/people, older adults, people aged XX and older.
INSTEAD OF: The elderly, old man/woman, old people, the aged, senior citizens, 'Boomers'.
WHY: Avoids outdated language and labels and assumptions of workforce participation based on age. Avoids generational terms unless referring to a specific generational study.

SAY THIS: Young/younger people, people aged XX-XX years.
INSTEAD OF: Junior or juniors, youths, 'Alphas'.
WHY: Avoids outdated language and labels. The term 'youths' can have negative connotations especially for young men.

SAY THIS: An effective and/or dynamic team, an experienced workforce.
INSTEAD OF: A young team, a middle-aged workforce.
WHY: Centres the attributes that drive success without highlighting age as a defining characteristic.

SAY THIS: Older workers, older employees, left the paid workforce, retired people or retirees.
INSTEAD OF: Mature workers.
WHY: Values diverse ageing experiences, emphasising ongoing contributions without imposing career endpoints. Recognises that stopping paid work doesn't define age or shared qualities. Note: There is no official retirement age in New Zealand and Australia. The age when a person completes paid work is a personal decision — and people who have stopped paid work do not necessarily share the same qualities or age group.

SAY THIS: 'Candidates should demonstrate proficiency in project management, strong leadership skills, and the ability to work collaboratively with diverse teams' for job descriptions.
INSTEAD OF: 10 years' experience is required.
WHY: Focuses on the skills and capabilities needed for the role which avoids age-related criteria that can lead to discrimination and overlooking qualified candidates.

SAY THIS: Person living with disability, person with lived experience of disability.
INSTEAD OF: (The) handicapped, (the) disabled, disabled people, cripple, invalid.
WHY: Emphasises the person first, rather than defining them solely by their disability. These are also outdated and negative terms.

SAY THIS: Person who has or lives with [name of condition or impairment].
INSTEAD OF: Afflicted by, suffers from, victim of, struck down.
WHY: Avoids framing the individual as passive or defined by their condition.

SAY THIS: Person who uses a wheelchair, wheelchair user.
INSTEAD OF: Confined to a wheelchair, wheelchair-bound.
WHY: Emphasises the person before their mobility aid, respecting their autonomy and recognising the wheelchair as a facilitating tool, not as a restriction or the focus of their identity.

SAY THIS: Person with a learning disability (singular), with learning disabilities (plural).
INSTEAD OF: Mentally handicapped, mentally defective, retarded, subnormal.
WHY: These are outdated and negative terms which are no longer appropriate to use.

SAY THIS: Person without a disability.
INSTEAD OF: Normal person, able-bodied, non-disabled person.
WHY: Avoids implying that not living with disability is the standard or superior state.

SAY THIS: Deaf, Sign Language, user of New Zealand Sign Language (NZSL), user of Australian Sign Language (Auslan), person with a hearing impairment.
INSTEAD OF: deaf (lowercase), deaf mute.
WHY: Respects the individual's identity and acknowledges their communication preferences and needs. Always capitalise 'Deaf' as this signifies a community that people identify with.

SAY THIS: Person with vision impairments, people who have low vision, person who is blind, person who is partially sighted.
INSTEAD OF: The blind.
WHY: Respects their individuality and acknowledges the range of vision impairments experienced within the community.

SAY THIS: Mobility parking, accessible parking/bathrooms/lifts. Accessibility action plans.
INSTEAD OF: 'Disabled' parking or bathrooms. Disability action plans.
WHY: Shifting the focus to accessibility promotes an inclusive environment for everyone.

SAY THIS: Victoria is neurotypical.
INSTEAD OF: Normal, of sound mind.
WHY: Accurately describes someone whose neurological development and state are typical.

SAY THIS: Karim has autism, or Karim is on the autism spectrum. Sam is autistic or Sam identifies as Autistic (if they identify that way).
INSTEAD OF: Aspy/aspie, profoundly autistic, mild autism. High functioning/low functioning.
WHY: Focuses on person-first language and recognises autism as one aspect of who they are. Prevents quantifying the neurodiversity of the individual. Note: 'Person first' language is generally more common in Australia. Respects individual preferences and acknowledges that many autistic people prefer identity-first language, viewing autism as an integral part of their identity. Note: 'Identity first' language is more commonly used in New Zealand.

SAY THIS: Li is neuroatypical/neurodiverse/neurodivergent.
INSTEAD OF: Mental/crazy.
WHY: Recognises the diversity of neurological conditions, emphasising there is no single 'normal' way for brains to function.

SAY THIS: Kai has ADHD.
INSTEAD OF: Hyper/hyperactive, space cadet.
WHY: Emphasises that ADHD is just one aspect of who Kai is.

SAY THIS: Morgan has a learning disability.
INSTEAD OF: Slow learner, stupid.
WHY: Respects and acknowledges that learning disabilities are just one part of a person's experience.


SAY THIS: Women, men, non-binary.
INSTEAD OF: Female, male.
WHY: In an everyday business environment, one is generally referring to a person's gender, rather than their sex because gender encompasses societal roles and identities. Refer to sex characteristics such as male or female only in contexts where these distinctions are specifically relevant such as medical and legal situations.

SAY THIS: Good morning everyone/team.
INSTEAD OF: Ladies and gentlemen/guys.
WHY: Gender-inclusive and respects gender identity.

SAY THIS: Partners, spouses.
INSTEAD OF: Boyfriend, girlfriend, husband, wife.
WHY: Respects diverse relationships and avoids heteronormative and outdated language terms.

SAY THIS: Chair/chairperson, salesperson, police officer.
INSTEAD OF: Chairman, salesman, policeman.
WHY: Avoids gender-specific titles.

SAY THIS: Step up, best person for the job.
INSTEAD OF: Man up, best man for the job.
WHY: Avoids perpetuating harmful gender stereotypes.

SAY THIS: Sexuality, sexual orientation.
INSTEAD OF: Sexual preference.
WHY: Avoids implying choice or preference in sexuality.

SAY THIS: Gender identity, man, woman.
INSTEAD OF: Decided to be a man or woman.
WHY: Gender identity is a deeply personal aspect of one's identity that may not always align with societal expectations or assigned sex at birth.

SAY THIS: Trans person, transgender person.
INSTEAD OF: Tranny/transvestite.
WHY: Avoids derogatory language. Transgender identities can be intersectional identities, just like any other. First Nations people who are trans in Australia might use the term Sistergirl or Brotherboy. In Aotearoa New Zealand, Maori people who are trans might identify as Tangata ira tane or Whakawahine.

SAY THIS: Transitioning.
INSTEAD OF: Getting a sex change.
WHY: Describes the process of transitioning gender.

SAY THIS: LGBTIQA+, Gay, lesbian, bisexual, takatapui.
INSTEAD OF: Homos/homosexuals/lesbos, the gays.
WHY: Uses respectful and inclusive language.

SAY THIS: Under-represented groups, Culturally and Linguistically Diverse (CALD) groups, Culturally and Racially Marginalised (CARM) groups.
INSTEAD OF: Minorities.
WHY: Acknowledges that certain communities may be marginalised or excluded based on factors beyond numerical representation. Who is under-represented depends on who has historically been in the dominant population, so this avoids 'othering'.

SAY THIS: Overseas professionals, international students.
INSTEAD OF: Foreigners, non-Australian/non-New Zealander etc.
WHY: Respects their nationality without implying otherness or foreignness.

SAY THIS: Immigrant, migrant, refugee, asylum seeker, person seeking safety.
INSTEAD OF: Illegal immigrant, illegal migrant, boat person.
WHY: Avoids dehumanisation and/or stigmatisation and respects Human Rights.

SAY THIS: What is your cultural background?
INSTEAD OF: 'Where are you from' or 'what are you?'
WHY: Allows the person to share their experiences and perspectives on their own terms.

SAY THIS: I don't understand it at all.
INSTEAD OF: It's all Greek to me.
WHY: Avoids outdated idioms that can be alienating and offensive.

SAY THIS: How do you prefer your name to be pronounced?
INSTEAD OF: That's a long name, that's a hard name to pronounce.
WHY: Shows respect for the individual by respecting their name as an essential part of their identity.

SAY THIS: Other racial groups, people of colour.
INSTEAD OF: Non-whites.
WHY: Avoids defining individuals by their racial status in relation to whiteness.

SAY THIS: Mixed ethnicity, biracial or multiracial people.
INSTEAD OF: Half-cast, exotic.
WHY: Respects the complexity of individuals' heritage and avoids exoticising or othering them.

SAY THIS: New parents/parents.
INSTEAD OF: 'Primary' and 'Secondary' carers.
WHY: Normalises care by both parents, reflecting the realities of care in working families. Feedback from parents is that the terms 'primary' and 'secondary' suggest that parents who are at work are less interested and involved in care for their children.

SAY THIS: Parents/guardians or carers/caregivers.
INSTEAD OF: Mother/father or mum/dad.
WHY: Acknowledges that caregiving roles are not limited to binary gender roles and includes diverse family structures, although some people may prefer to be called 'mum' or 'dad'. Is inclusive of legal guardians, stepparents, foster parents, or other caregivers who may not be biologically related to the child. Acknowledges that caregiving extends beyond biological or legal parenthood.

SAY THIS: 'Single parent family' or 'one parent family' or 'solo caregiver'.
INSTEAD OF: 'Broken home' or 'single parent'.
WHY: Acknowledges non-traditional family structures that may not fit the nuclear family ideal.
