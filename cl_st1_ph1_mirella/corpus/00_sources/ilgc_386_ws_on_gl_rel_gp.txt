APPLICATIONS OF THE POLICY

LANGUAGE ABOUT HUMAN BEINGS

The primary focus of this policy is on terms that refer to people. Gender-inclusive diction avoids the generic use of terms such as "man" (also "men," "mankind," "family of man," "city of man," "brotherhood," "clergyman," etc.). In their place, terms such as "human," "human being," "people," "humanity," "humankind," "earthly city," "mortals," "member of the clergy," etc. can be used to designate individuals and groups. The possibilities for gracefully rephrasing one's writing and speaking, once one develops the habit of doing so, are amazingly diverse.

PRONOUNS

The generic use of the masculine pronouns "he," "his," and "him" is also avoided in gender-inclusive usage. There are several strategies available for avoiding the generic masculine pronoun: recasting the sentence in the plural ("students... they" rather than "the student... he"); using paired pronouns ("he or she," "his or her"); rewriting the sentence so that no pronoun is needed; or using "one" where appropriate. The singular use of "they" is increasingly accepted in both spoken and written English and may also be used.

LANGUAGE ABOUT GOD

Language about God raises distinct questions. The Christian tradition affirms that God transcends human categories, including gender, even though scripture and tradition have most often spoken of God in masculine terms. Gender-inclusive usage seeks to avoid the impression that God is male: for example, by repeating the word "God" rather than using masculine pronouns, by using the reflexive form "Godself," or by drawing on the rich variety of images and titles for God found in scripture and the tradition. Writers and speakers are encouraged to expand their vocabulary of divine imagery rather than relying exclusively on masculine terms.

LANGUAGE ABOUT JESUS CHRIST

The maleness of Jesus of Nazareth is a historical particularity, and masculine pronouns are appropriate when referring to the historical person of Jesus. Care should be taken, however, in theological statements about the risen Christ, the second person of the Trinity, or the divine Word, where exclusively masculine language may obscure the theological point being made.

TRINITARIAN LANGUAGE

The traditional formula "Father, Son, and Holy Spirit" remains normative in creedal, liturgical, and sacramental contexts. In other contexts, writers may also draw on complementary trinitarian images from scripture and tradition.

QUOTATIONS

Direct quotations from published sources, including scripture, liturgical texts, and historical documents, are not altered to conform to this policy. Writers may, however, choose translations and editions that use gender-inclusive language, and may paraphrase in inclusive terms where a direct quotation is not required.

BIBLICAL TRANSLATIONS

When quoting scripture, students and faculty are encouraged to use translations that employ gender-inclusive language for human beings, such as the New Revised Standard Version (NRSV).
