Preferred Language | Language to Avoid | Why is it preferred?

1) Identity First Language, Autistic Individual
   Person First Language, Person with Autism
   Emphasises autism as an integral part of a person's identity. Individuals may prefer to identify with their neurodivergence. Respects the neurotype and avoids framing autism as a disorder or "condition." However, some neurodivergent people differ in their language preference on identity first language. Acknowledging each individual's preferred language is respectful toward their identity and lived experiences.

2) Autism, Autistic
   Has Autism, Has Asperger's, Has Asperger's Syndrome, Asperger's, Asperger's Syndrome, On the spectrum, With autism, AS/ASD, Autism Spectrum Disorder, Autism Spectrum, ASC/Autism Spectrum Condition, Suffers from autism, Classic Autistic
   Moving away from the view that autism is a disorder that someone "has" towards respecting different neurotypes. Asperger's or Asperger's syndrome is no longer an official clinical diagnosis, and people may be assessed to determine if they are autistic, and if so, confirmed as autistic. The history behind the formerly diagnostic term 'Asperger's' forms part of the context for moving away from this terminology. The term "Asperger's" derived from the work of Hans Asperger, a psychiatrist who worked with the Nazis and employed abhorrent descriptions of autistic children. Amanda McGuinness explains why "on the spectrum" or "with autism" are not helpful.

3) Autism Class
   ASD Unit, Autism Unit, Unit
   Children and adults, regardless of their neurotype, are taught in classrooms, not units. There is no requirement or need for a school to use the DSM-5 term "ASD" when referring to Autistic students. Avoid abbreviations that dehumanise.

4) Disability, Difference
   Disorder
   Disability is not a 'dirty' word. People should be free to choose to declare themselves disabled, receive the support they need, and not be shamed or be reacted to negatively for doing so either from individuals or societal structures. "Disability" or "Difference" encourages a move towards a more inclusive and respectful approach to neurodiversity. Advocates for reclaiming and owning the terms "disabled" and "disability" as neutral descriptors of difference and identity challenge stigma and promote disability pride and empowerment. "Disorder" suggests there is something inherently wrong or dysfunctional about a person.

5) Differences, Challenges, Needs
   Deficits/Impairment
   Highlights the uniqueness of neurodivergent individuals without framing their challenges or characteristics as deficits or impairments. Using "difference" emphasises the value of neurodiversity and promotes a more positive and inclusive perspective, focusing on strengths rather than limitations. "Differences" recognises that diverse ways of thinking, perceiving, and experiencing the world contribute to the richness of human diversity and should be celebrated. Using "challenges" recognises that there are disabling environments and barriers arising from systems and practices designed to neuronormative standards.

6) Individuals with specific support requirements; Individuals with diverse needs; Individuals with a disability; Disabled person; Neurodivergent; Additional Needs; Complex support needs
   Special Needs, Special (for example, special education or special class)
   The phrase "special needs" can cause harm in several ways. First, disability accommodations are not special, and framing them that way may increase stigma. Second, euphemisms for disability make it seem like disability is a shameful word. Referring to "special needs" can infantilise people. Their needs and rights are not extra or special but are derived from the same fundamental civil rights of all people. Framing a person or their needs as special fosters segregation.

7) Autistic or Neurodivergent development trajectory
   Regression, Missing Milestones, Developmental Delay, Problematic development, Developmental difficulties
   "Different development trajectory" acknowledges that neurodivergent individuals may follow unique paths of growth and change that are not predictable according to traditional perspectives. This language recognises that development is ongoing, and multifaceted, and acknowledges the unique experience of many neurodivergent individuals in their ongoing development. "Regression" or missing milestones implies backward development or lack of progress, which can be stigmatising for neurodivergent individuals who experience periods of developmental change that diverge from typical expectations. Neurodivergent children may experience challenges with basic needs such as toileting/feeding.

8) Support, Accommodations, Adjustments
   Treatment, Cures, Therapy to "cure", Interventions, Fix autism/Cure autism/Treat autism
   Using this language emphasises the need to create environments that support and accommodate diverse needs and promote equitable participation. The language recognises neurodiversity as a natural aspect of human variation and focuses on quality of life and internal wellbeing. On the other hand, "treatment/cures" or "interventions" suggest a deficit-oriented and pathologising approach, implying that neurodivergent individuals need fixing or curing. This can reinforce stigma and undermines self-acceptance and inclusion.

9) Non-speaking, Speaking at times
   Non-verbal, Pre-verbal
   "Non-speaking" is preferred because it accurately describes individuals who primarily communicate through methods other than spoken language, such as sign language, gestures, or augmentative and alternative communication (AAC). This term recognises the diverse ways non-speaking individuals communicate, without suggesting they don't communicate at all. In contrast, "non-verbal" might wrongly imply a total lack of communication ability, which is not accurate for those who use alternative forms of communication effectively. Using "non-speaking" respects the various communication methods of these neurodivergent individuals.

10) Characteristic
   Symptoms, Signs, Impairments, Deficit, Difficulties attributed to being autistic
   Characteristic is the preferred term because it acknowledges that neurodiversity is an inherent aspect of an individual's identity, personality, or cognitive profile. This term supports the individual uniqueness of neurodivergent people, highlighting their strengths and challenges. On the other hand, "signs/symptoms" may imply a medicalised perspective, framing neurodiversity as a collection of problems or challenges to be addressed through treatment or curing. Neuro-affirmative practices reject the notion of treating or curing autism, instead focusing on understanding, accepting, and accommodating neurodiverse characteristics.

11) Neurotypical
   Normal
   "Neurotypical" is the preferred term because it accurately describes individuals who do not have neurological differences, without implying superiority or normality. This term recognises the diversity of neurological profiles and avoids stigmatising language that suggests deviations from a norm. On the other hand, "Normal" may imply a standard or ideal, reinforcing "societal norms" and marginalising neurodivergent individuals.

12) Intellectual disability, Developmental disability
   Re*ard, Handicapped
   Using the preferred language respects the dignity and humanity of individuals with cognitive differences and acknowledges the challenges they may face without resorting to derogatory language. "Re*ard" is deeply offensive and harmful, and perpetuates hurtful stereotypes, and devalues the inherent worth and humanity of individuals with intellectual or developmental disabilities. 'Handicapped' is outdated, and can cause offence to individuals with disabilities as it carries negative connotations and focuses on limitations, not abilities.

13) Sensory Processing Differences, Sensory Differences, Autistic Sensory Perception
   Sensory Processing Disorder, Sensory Abnormalities
   Each individual has a unique way of processing information and experiencing the environment around them. Differences in sensory experiences are natural and should not be seen as deviations from a perceived norm. "Disorder" suggests there is something inherently wrong or dysfunctional about how an individual processes information and reacts to the environment. Using the preferred language embraces diversity in how neurodivergent individuals perceive and interact with the environment, and supports differences in sensory experiences creating an inclusive environment.

14) Support Needs
   High/Low Functioning, Mild/severe
   "Support Needs" is preferred because it recognises the individualised supports or accommodations required to address the unique strengths, challenges, and preferences of neurodivergent individuals. This term avoids categorising individuals based on functioning labels, which can oversimplify and stigmatise their experiences.

15) Dysregulated, Meltdown
   Disruptive, Bold, Behavioural Outburst, Challenging Behaviour, Behaviour of concern, Tantrum, other critical terms
   "Dysregulated" is the preferred term because it accurately describes a state of imbalance in the central nervous system or instability in emotional or sensory regulation without using stigmatising language. This term recognises the challenges faced by neurodivergent individuals in managing their emotions or responses to stimuli, and acknowledges that neurodivergent people often experience environments differently to others. Meltdown can occur when a neurodivergent person is overwhelmed in response to stimuli and the term signals nervous system distress. In contrast, critical terms may imply a lack of self-discipline or a view that the behaviour is chosen, which can reinforce misconceptions and negative stereotypes.

16) Passion, Passionate, Hobby, Areas of strength, Area of expertise, Hyperfocus, Attention to detail, Focussed interest
   Obsessive, Obsessed, Special Interest, Rigid interest, Repetitive interest
   Having a passion for and being passionate about something are preferred terms as they convey the deep interest and strong focus a neurodivergent individual may have for a particular topic, item, activity etc. Conversely, using words "obsessive" or "obsessed" about an interest suggests an unhealthy fixation on that interest, which disregards the happiness and self-regulatory capacity of the particular interest. Special interest implies that there is something unusual about the neurodivergent person's hobby.

17) Authentic communication
   Social Skills
   Neurotypical social skills training is fully rejected by the neurodiversity paradigm, where comparisons to the practice of gay conversion therapy are made. Like all behaviour-based models, neurotypical social skills training encourages neurodivergent people to mask or hide their true selves (which decades of psychological research have shown us is a core element of positive mental health) and instead promotes masking. It leads to internalised feelings of shame and the person's core ways of being and interacting are presented as something to hide and change. It ignores Milton's double empathy: any difficulties in communication are mutual and are not located in the neurodivergent person alone.

18) World Autism Day, World Autism Month
   Autism Acceptance Day, Autism Acceptance Month
   We advocate for moving from Autism Acceptance Day and Autism Acceptance Month to recognising "World Autism Day" and "World Autism Month". Autistic people should not have to seek anyone's 'acceptance'. Rather than placing the onus on autistic people to seek acceptance, we encourage non autistic people to educate themselves on autism, and what supports autistic people might need so as to be included within communities.
