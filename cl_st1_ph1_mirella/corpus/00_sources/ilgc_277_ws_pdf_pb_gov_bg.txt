INCLUSIVE COMMUNICATION GUIDE
Office of LGBTIQA+ Affairs 

INTRODUCTION

Who is this guide for?
This guide was developed for use by the ACT Government. Where relevant, it can be useful for NGOs, corporates and anyone else seeking this guidance.

What is inclusive communication?

Inclusive communication refers to written, verbal and visual communications that actively include people and do not leave anyone out. Using inclusive communication means not making assumptions about the people you are talking to or about.

Why does inclusive communication matter?
The language we use shapes how we see the world and has an impact on those we communicate with. Inclusive communication is a way for us to create environments where people feel included, recognised, and respected.

Inclusive communication is important because it is accurate. It ensures that we are not perpetuating stereotypes, making generalisations, reinforcing harmful gender norms, or marginalising individuals based on their gender identity and expression, sexual orientation, or sex characteristics.

LGBTIQA+ people are a statistically significant population group whose experiences should be represented. The most recent data shows that 5.6% of the ACT population are LGBTI+.
For LGBTIQA+ individuals, the use of inclusive communication signals acknowledgment of their existence and respects their human rights. The ACT is a human rights jurisdiction, as legislated under the Human Rights Act 2004. This legislation provides a framework for the protection of human rights in the ACT, which includes protected attributes such as gender, sexuality, and sex characteristics.

The ACT Government is committed to advancing the rights, health, wellbeing and other positive outcomes for all people living in the ACT through the implementation of a range of plans and strategies, such as the ACT Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Agreement, the ACT Disability Strategy (and related sub-strategies), the ACT Women's Plan, the Charter for Multiculturalism (under the Multiculturalism Act 2023), the ACT Work Health and Safety Act, and the Capital of Equality Strategy. Promoting inclusive communication for ACT Public Service staff is an action of the First Action Plan (2024–2026) of the Capital of Equality Strategy (2024–2029).
When explaining the need for inclusive communication, you could say that it matters because:

It helps all people feel included.
It matters to LGBTIQA+ people and acknowledges their experiences.
We are more accurately describing the complexity of human reality.
We are actively acknowledging the existence of people who have long been erased.
We want to reach otherwise hard-to-reach populations.
We don't want to alienate a group of service users.

Language changes
Language is not static. It varies from culture to culture. The way our ancestors spoke differs from how we speak now. What may be considered inclusive today could change as society's understanding grows and perspectives shift over time, including with new evidence. It is important for us and the systems we design to remain flexible and open about changes in language and terminology. Change also often emerges first within communities, which means that government systems might not immediately reflect this change. When individuals are asked to describe which terms they use for themselves, such as selecting their gender on a survey, there should be flexibility around available options. A free text option to allow for maximum flexibility is recommended.

If mistakes are made
Inclusive communication is about continuous learning and openness to change. Mistakes happen. What matters is how we respond to making mistakes. When using incorrect language (such as misgendering someone), it is important not to get defensive, but to acknowledge the mistake, correct it, and continue with an inclusive mindset. Apologising without excessively dwelling on it is a key part of maintaining respect without shifting the burden of discomfort onto the affected person.
When making a mistake, remember these four simple steps:

Acknowledge it.
Correct it.
Don't dwell on it.
Learn from it.
