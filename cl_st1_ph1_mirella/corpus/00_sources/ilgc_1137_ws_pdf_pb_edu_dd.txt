For example, uses a wheelchair rather than confined to a wheelchair.

Say people with intellectual disabilities. Do not use retarded, mentally retarded or subnormal. (See "Rosa's Law and the Language of Bullying" for more details.)

Use person with a brain injury or person who has a traumatic brain injury. Do not say brain damaged.

Use person with cancer or people who have AIDS. Do not use the term victim, e.g., AIDS victim.

Use person with a congenital disability or person with a disability since birth. Birth defect and deformity are considered offensive.

Say deaf woman or hard of hearing boy. People who have some degree of hearing and vision loss prefer the term Deaf-Blind. Never use deaf and dumb or deaf mute.

Say she has cerebral palsy, he has a developmental disability or he is autistic. Do not say mentally retarded.

Say person with Down syndrome. Do not use mongoloid or Down person.

Say person with a learning disability. Do not use slow learner or retarded.

Say person with post-polio syndrome. Do not use polio victim.

Say girl with epilepsy or teen with a seizure disorder. Do not use epileptic fit or spastic attacks.

For a person without speech capability, say non-speaking or person without speech. Do not use mute or dumb.

While people with spinal cord injuries may refer to themselves as a para or a quad, communicators should use man with paraplegia, woman with paralysis or person with a spinal cord injury. Don't say cripple or handicapped.

Although such terms as alcoholic and addict are medically acceptable, some individuals may consider them derogatory. More acceptable terms are people who are substance dependent or person who is alcohol dependent.
