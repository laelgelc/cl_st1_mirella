INCLUSIVE COMMUNICATION RECOMMENDATIONS

Use gender-neutral language
Gender-neutral language promotes inclusion of all genders by not making assumptions about people. It is useful in contexts where there is no need to specify gender. Where possible, opt for gender-neutral language so nobody is left out. Remember, gender-neutral language should be used in the absence of specific instructions. Should someone prefer to be referred to by a gendered term, that is okay too.

Also use gender-neutral terms to refer to professions. Historically, many professions have been referred to using masculine terms, which can inadvertently suggest that these roles are predominantly or exclusively for men. Replace these gendered terms with gender-neutral options or a different term.

Using gender-neutral language also helps reject a binary understanding of gender and instead signals recognition of and respect for gender diversity. For example, naming something as 'for women' is not simply about the target audience but rather about the intent: addressing gender inequality or sexism. These policies and services can be relevant for people who are not women but who also experience gender inequality or sexism.

Know: Framing domestic, family and sexual violence (DFSV) exclusively as 'violence against women' can prevent people who are not cisgender women from recognising that they are in abusive relationships and from accessing support. It also carries the risk of rendering DFSV (including coercive control) in LGBTIQA+ relationships invisible to first responders and service providers. Evidence shows that DFSV is driven by gender inequality. Gender inequality in turn both negatively impacts LGBTIQA+ people and drives violence against these communities.
Saying 'this policy impacts women' can unintentionally exclude non-binary or gender-diverse people who face similar issues. It is also important to remember that not everyone who benefits from work described as 'for women' may use that term for themselves.
Moving beyond an understanding of gender as a binary means actively including certain people, such as, but not limited to, agender, non-binary, and genderfluid people.
In some cultural contexts, such as for ceremony and cultural practices for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, use of binary language occurs (i.e. 'men's' or 'women's business') and must be respected.


Use the terms 'women' and 'men' in an inclusive way
In some contexts, it may be necessary to preserve the use of gendered terms such as 'woman' and 'man'. In such instances, use these terms in an inclusive way to recognise that women and men are diverse and that their experiences can vary based on factors like sexuality, race, or disability. Use of 'woman' and 'man' should also not exclude certain people, such as transgender people. Excluding transgender women from women's spaces or services and transgender men from men's spaces or services is discrimination and violates their human rights.

Use the term 'non-binary' in meaningful ways
There is no single way to be non-binary. Non-binary people have varied identities and may feel masculine, feminine, neither, both, or genderfluid. While some non-binary individuals may feel comfortable with femininity — others do not. Thinking of some non-binary people as feminine and others as not creates a division between non-binary people and pushes them back into a binary gender framework, which they often seek to move beyond.

Some non-binary people see themselves as transgender, while others do not. Additionally, some may be comfortable associating themselves with gendered terms like motherhood or fatherhood, while others may not. Importantly, you cannot tell if someone is non-binary just by their appearance.

Exercise caution when using wording such as 'woman and non-binary people' without specifying. If you want to engage with people who have experienced negative impacts of sexism and want to be inclusive, then be specific and use 'women, including non-binary and/or gender-diverse people with lived experience of misogyny or sexism'.

However, if you claim that your service or event is simply for 'women and non-binary people,' you need to take active steps to make that inclusion real. This means doing the necessary work to ensure that people of all gender presentations — whether masculine, feminine, androgynous, or otherwise — are welcomed and treated equally.

The same guidance applies to the use of the term 'gender-diverse people'.
Know: Phrases like 'women, men and non-binary people' are not inclusive by default because they create unnecessary rigid categories that come across as mutually exclusive. Where necessary and appropriate, specify exactly who you are after (e.g. people with lived experience of sexism).

Use the standardised LGBTIQA+ acronym
We use 'LGBTIQA+' as the standard acronym in all ACT Government communications. It stands for lesbian, gay, bisexual, trans, intersex, queer, asexual, aromantic and agender people. By including the plus sign, we also include people who are pansexual, non-binary, gender diverse, and remain open to more identities, expressions and experiences.

It is crucial to recognise that there is no single experience or narrative that captures what it means to be part of the LGBTIQA+ communities. The terms we use provide a common language, but they cannot encompass the full breadth and complexity of individual identities and experiences.
Know: A in the acronym stands for three distinct communities: asexual, aromantic and agender people.

Acknowledge diversity among LGBTIQA+ communities
Each letter in the LGBTIQA+ acronym refers to a different community. Although some challenges are shared, each of these communities faces their unique set of struggles. It is important to acknowledge the diversity within LGBTIQA+ communities.
Avoid → Use:

LGBTIQA+ community (in singular) → LGBTIQA+ communities (in plural)

Being LGBTIQA+ is not confined to one stage of life — it spans across childhood, adolescence, adulthood, and older age — and inclusive communication must reflect this reality.
For children and young people, inclusive communication helps create environments where they feel safe to express and explore themselves. It validates their experiences, promotes self-acceptance, and reduces the risk of bullying and exclusion.

For older LGBTIQA+ people, inclusive communication acknowledges their lifelong experiences, including the historical discrimination they may have faced. It ensures they feel seen and respected in areas like aged care, healthcare, and community services, where they are often overlooked or misrepresented. You should never assume that older people no longer identify with or prioritise their LGBTIQA+ status.

Any person regardless of their race, culture, ethnicity, religion or disability can be LGBTIQA+. We recommend you adopt an intersectional lens in policy development, communications and service delivery. An intersectional lens helps understand that people's experiences are shaped by multiple factors, including race, ethnicity, disability, socioeconomic status, migration status and more. This helps you avoid one-size-fits-all descriptions or approaches.

Know: Many people have overlapping experiences of discrimination that are not limited to one identity or experience. For example, a queer person with disability may face additional barriers to healthcare access compared to a queer person who does not live with disability. You should never assume that someone's experience of discrimination is limited to just one aspect of their identity.

Expand inclusive terminology
The term SOGIESC — which stands for sexual orientation, gender identity and expression, and sex characteristics — has emerged as an alternative to the term LGBTIQA+. Rather than referring to communities, this term refers to parts of people's identities, expressions or sex characteristics. It is useful because we can write about people's identities and include people who are not a part of LGBTIQA+ communities or who do not feel represented by the LGBTIQA+ acronym (e.g., because of its Western origin or when cultural descriptors are used). The broad nature of the term SOGIESC is helpful because it refers to social concepts rather than specific communities. This makes it less likely that more letters will be added to include groups that might be feeling left out within the current LGBTIQA+ acronym.

Another useful phrase is 'sexual, gender, bodily, relational, and familial diversity'. This phrase encompasses a broad range of identities and experiences, also ensuring that discussions are not limited to just LGBTIQA+ individuals but also include diverse forms of relationships and family structures, such as those in polyamorous relationships.
Examples:

This proposal addresses issues related to discrimination based on SOGIESC status.
Our organisation is committed to fostering an inclusive and respectful environment where all individuals, regardless of their sexual orientation, gender identity, gender expression, or sex characteristics (SOGIESC), are supported.
Referring to community in this resource, we include all people in their sexual, gender, bodily, relational, and familial diversity.


Understand and use cultural terms
The LGBTIQA+ acronym emerged within a Western cultural context. While people known as LGBTIQA+ today have existed throughout history across the world, they have often been known by a range of different cultural terms. These terms are valid and if someone prefers a cultural term, then that should be used.

The existence of cultural terms relating to sexual, gender, and bodily diversity is another example of why free text entry is a good option when it comes to designing surveys and questionnaires. It is also an example of how the acronym SOGIESC can be more inclusive than LGBTIQA+.

Accurately represent people with innate variations in sex characteristics
People born with innate variations in sex characteristics, also referred to as intersex people, are often left out and erased. If you use the LGBTIQA+ acronym (or any version that includes the 'I' for intersex), then you should remember that you are talking about issues related to sexual orientation, gender identity and expression, and, importantly, sex characteristics.
If you use the LGBTIQA+ acronym, but only talk about 'issues relating to sexual orientation and gender identity' or expression, this erases intersex people and their struggles. If you are not specifically considering issues experienced by intersex people in your work on LGBTIQA+ communities, it is best to be transparent about it. In these instances, some use the LGBTQA+ acronym (without the 'I').

People with variations in sex characteristics are a diverse population and do not share a single identity. Like anyone else, they have diverse genders, and some may be also trans and gender diverse, and/or have diverse sexual orientations. Others might be heterosexual and cisgender.
Individuals with variations in sex characteristics may use different terms to describe themselves, such as being intersex, having an intersex variation or condition, having an innate variation of sex characteristics, or may use the specific term relating to their variation. While some may use the term intersex for community and human rights advocacy, not all view it as an identity.

Know: Diagnostic and medicalising language should be avoided when speaking about intersex people, unless absolutely relevant, such as in a medical setting. 'LGBTQA' refers to matters of sexual orientation, gender identity and expression.

Do not presume heterosexuality as the norm
When it comes to sexuality, we encourage people not to presume that everyone is heterosexual, which erases the existence of lesbian, gay, bisexual, queer, asexual and many more people. The assumption that heterosexuality is the norm — also called heteronormativity — leads to assumptions about relationships and family structures and invalidates real life experiences.
Avoid → Use:

What is your wife's name? (in the context of asking a man without knowing the gender of his partner) → What is your partner's name?
What is the name of Bobby's father? (asked of a parent, in the context of childcare, assuming that every child has a mother and a father) → Is there another parent you want to list? What is their name?

While sexuality should not be equated with sexual activity, assumptions that everyone is heterosexual leads to assumptions that everyone engages in sexual activity with people of another gender. This can be particularly harmful in healthcare settings.
Examples:

While ordering a pregnancy test for patients marked as women on medical records may be a standard practice, doing so automatically and assuming heterosexuality is unnecessary and invalidating. Instead ask your patient if they could be pregnant without any assumptions or judgment.
There may be contexts in which trans men, gender diverse, or non-binary people are denied pregnancy screenings or abortion care — such as in situations of sexual assault — because of harmful heteronormative assumptions. Ask your patient if they could be pregnant without any assumptions or judgment.
Refusing to order an STI screening for a patient who is a woman because she disclosed that she is in a relationship with another woman can lead to harmful health outcomes and deny people the care they need.
Discussing sexual intercourse after birth only in relation to penetrative sex fails to provide tailored health advice for people after birth at physio appointments. Respectfully ask your patients about their sexual partners and tailor your advice.

Recognise asexual and aromantic communities
Acknowledge that there are people who are asexual or aromantic.
Asexual individuals experience little to no sexual attraction. Asexual individuals may still experience other forms of attraction, such as romantic, emotional, or intellectual, and can have fulfilling relationships and connections. Asexuality is also a spectrum comprising multiple other identities such as demisexual and others. An asexual person might still have sex (e.g. for work).

An aromantic person experiences little or no romantic attraction to others. Aromantic people may still form deep and meaningful relationships that are not based on romance. Understand that these identities exist on a spectrum and that they can overlap.
Inclusive communication helps validate these experiences and ensures they are not excluded from considerations in policy and services, or our communications.
Avoid → Use:

Language that assumes everyone experiences sexual or romantic attraction → Language which challenges stereotypes and misconceptions about asexual and aromantic people, such as the idea that they "haven't met the right person yet"
Assuming that everyone experiences sexual or romantic attraction → Inclusive phrases such as "people with different types of attraction or relationships"

Know:

When collecting information about sexual orientation, include asexuality as an option.
Ensure examples in policies (e.g., caregiving or personal leave) do not always prioritise romantic or sexual relationships and consider other forms of connections, like friendships, support networks or chosen families.
When describing relationships or attraction, ensure examples include asexual and aromantic experiences, such as friendships, chosen families, or intellectual partnerships.


Share pronouns
In the English language we use pronouns in speech and written communications every day. Making assumptions about other people's pronouns, though, can result in misgendering, which is harmful to trans, gender-diverse and non-binary people. Wherever possible, make the sharing of pronouns part of your everyday practice, especially if you are not trans, gender diverse, or non-binary yourself. Making sharing pronouns a part of everyday life helps normalise the use of them and means that trans, gender-diverse, and non-binary people do not have to do all the heavy lifting alone.
How to make pronouns a part of everyday life:

Include them in your email signature and on Teams.
Add a pronoun pin on your lanyard.
Add pronouns to name badges.
Make them part of your introduction, such as: 'Hi, my name is [name]. My pronouns are [pronouns].'
Ask pronouns in forms and surveys, if relevant.

It is preferable to share your own pronouns first and give others space to share theirs at a time and in a way they feel comfortable, rather than directly asking someone for their pronouns. When talking about pronouns, avoid using the word 'preferred', as pronouns are not a preference.
Know: Here are suggestions on how to encourage the sharing of pronouns at your workplace without making this process seem exceptional:

It is your choice to share your pronouns. If you do, this can help foster an inclusive and respectful environment for everyone.
We invite you to share your pronouns to help create a space where all of us are respected.
You're welcome to share your pronouns.
Sharing your pronouns is welcome, so everyone can be respected and acknowledged.

When using pronouns in the context of policy work or HR documents (e.g. recruitment or probation reports), it is recommended to opt for a gender-neutral 'they/them' rather than using gendered options.
Avoid → Use:

Have any weaknesses been drawn to his/her attention? (example from a Probation report) → Have any weaknesses been drawn to their attention? (example from a Probation report)

There are many different pronouns. The most common ones in English are: she/her, he/him, they/them. A person may have any combination of these, e.g. they/she, or he/they. For some people who use multiple pronouns, the sequence in which they list them might indicate their preference. For others, the sequence does not necessarily indicate preference but other considerations, such as practicality.
Some people also use 'neopronouns'. These are pronouns that are not part of the standard pronouns in a language and instead form part of a new language developed by trans and gender diverse people that is gender neutral. Some commonly used neopronouns are: xe/xir/xirs, se/sir/sirs, fae/faer/faers, ey/em/eir.
Where possible, we encourage the use of a free-text box entry in surveys and forms, to allow for maximum flexibility regarding pronoun choice.

Address groups in inclusive ways
When addressing a group of people, it is important to avoid binary and unnecessarily gendered language. Addressing a group of people with gendered terms means making assumptions about everyone in the group and can result in the exclusion and/or misgendering of trans, non-binary, gender-diverse, and agender people.
Where gender is unclear, use 'Dear [First and last name]'. Other gender-neutral options include using a generic salutation or leaving the space blank. Waiting until you know the person's form of address is always the most respectful approach. Do not assume someone uses 'Mrs.' simply because they are married. Use of Mx does not automatically imply that person belongs to the LGBTIQA+ communities. Anyone can use Mx, which is a gender-neutral form of address where the 'x' replaces the letters that signify gender (such as Ms or Mr).

Refer to medical procedures and body parts in an inclusive way
Health service providers may use heavily gendered language that excludes or creates significant discomfort for trans, gender-diverse, and non-binary people, as well as people of diverse sexualities. Many trans, gender-diverse, and non-binary people avoid seeking medical support, due to past bad experiences in healthcare settings, leading to overall poor health outcomes. It is important to acknowledge that not all individuals who access healthcare services have the gender traditionally associated with certain procedures or body parts.

Health service providers may also use language that presumes heterosexuality. Having to out oneself can make people avoid medical appointments out of fear that they might be judged or discriminated against for not being heterosexual.

Using inclusive language in a health service setting signals that the service welcomes trans, gender-diverse, and non-binary people, as well as people of diverse sexualities, leading to overall better engagement with the service and improved health outcomes for these populations.
Remember: List the medical procedure without assuming the gender of the person who needs it!

Remember: For medical conditions and body parts, list them without exclusively associating a particular gender with it.

WORDS AND PHRASES TO AVOID
Avoid using 'preferred'
While use of 'preferred' is still relatively commonplace, we encourage people to move past this word. The phrases 'preferred pronouns', 'preferred gender', and 'preferred sexuality' imply that a person's pronouns, gender, or sexuality are optional or subject to choice by others. Simply saying 'pronouns', 'gender', or 'sexuality' avoids these assumptions and shows respect. It acknowledges that the person sharing this information is best positioned to define it.
Similarly, 'preferred name' can imply a choice rather than respecting someone's actual name. The use of 'correct' name is another option in certain contexts.
Occasionally, there is a need to make a distinction between legal name and chosen name. In these cases, make sure that the chosen name is the one visible to the person and used in interactions with them. It may also be beneficial to have an explanation readily available as to why legal name must be collected.


Avoid using 'identify as'
While use of 'identify as' is still relatively commonplace, we encourage people to move past this. Like the term 'preferred', 'identify' suggests choice. Saying 'all who identify as women' implies that some people may not really be women but are only identifying as such, which is not respectful. The same applies for sexuality.


Avoid using terms 'male' and 'female' unless necessary
The terms 'female' or 'male' have strong biomedical associations and are often used to exclude trans and gender diverse people. Sometimes these terms are used as direct quotes from research which only collected sex rather than gender. If this is the case, try to limit their use to direct quotes only.
We generally recommend using the gendered terms 'woman' and 'man' to replace the use of 'female' and 'male'. Sometimes this means that you will use 'woman' and 'man' as adjectives. If this does not offer a good reading flow, we suggest restructuring your sentence.


Avoid creating false opposites
When comparing or contrasting groups, avoid using 'heterosexual' or 'straight' as the opposite of LGBTIQA+. People born with variations in sex characteristics or transgender individuals can also be heterosexual. Instead, use more precise language that recognises the diversity within LGBTIQA+ communities.

Know: If you are comparing some findings based on sexual orientation only, it is better to say heterosexual people versus those with other or diverse sexual orientations.

Avoid marginalising language
Some terms for LGBTIQA+ people can come across as disempowering. Try to use terms for LGBTIQA+ communities that do not reinforce a sense of otherness.

Avoid a limited view on inclusion
The word 'tolerance' implies that the presence or behaviour of certain individuals is merely tolerated rather than accepted or embraced. This can suggest a begrudging acceptance rather than genuine inclusion, respect and belonging. Use terms that reflect a celebration of diversity.


VISUAL REPRESENTATION
Use of LGBTIQA+ flags and symbols
There are many flags and symbols commonly associated with LGBTIQA+ communities.
The rainbow flag is globally recognised as a collective representation of LGBTIQA+ communities. It is also referred to as the 'pride flag'. In its common use it consists of 6 coloured stripes starting with red and followed by orange, yellow, green, blue and purple.
In recent years, multiple versions have been created to reflect community feedback and represent voices marginalised within the communities.
The 'Philadelphia Pride Flag' adds black and brown stripes to the pride flag to specifically include Black, First Nations and other people of colour who are LGBTIQA+. This flag was designed to acknowledge that the movement for LGBTIQA+ rights has started and been led by First Nations, Black communities, and communities of colour.
There is also the 'Progress Pride Flag', of which there are three versions:

This version adds a five-striped chevron to represent marginalised communities of colour and to honour transgender communities (in blue, pink, and white stripes).
This version adds a flag to represent intersex communities.
This version features an umbrella to represent the sex worker community, which has historically been one of the few available and reliable forms of work for many marginalised LGBTIQA+ people.

If you want to represent the LGBTIQA+ communities in their diversity, we recommend you use the Philadelphia Pride Flag, a trans communities' flag and an intersex communities flag as three standalone flags.

Know: The circle on the intersex communities' flag needs to remain unbroken as it symbolises wholeness and ongoing fight for bodily autonomy for people with variations in sex characteristics.

If you wish to use the Progress Pride Flag, you can. However, we recommend you use the intersex communities' flag and if relevant the symbol for sex workers — the red umbrella — as stand-alone.
It is also important to recognise that different communities have their own flags. When referring to only a specific LGBTIQA+ community, use the relevant flag for that community, where one exists.
When designing materials for publication or presentation, it is important not to take the liberty to design new flags or symbols to represent LGBTIQA+ communities. Designing new flags or symbols without community consent is offensive and potentially very damaging. It is also important not to change the order of the colours, the tone of the colours, or shapes used on the original flags.
Know: Make sure to consult with the Office of LGBTIQA+ Affairs on the latest applicable flags and symbols.

Challenge stereotypes in visual representation
Stereotypes in imagery can reinforce harmful assumptions about LGBTIQA+ people and exclude those with diverse experiences.
