Gender, Gender Identity, and Sexual Orientation
Before discussing gender bias, it is important to distinguish between sex and gender. Sexual orientation refers to romantic, emotional, or sexual attraction, while gender identity relates to how individuals perceive themselves, which may or may not align with conventional categories. Sex and gender should not be used interchangeably.

Respecting gender identity means using language that honours how individuals see themselves. Biased language marginalises those with nontraditional identities or orientations.

Common Guidelines

Avoid masculine pronouns for general references .
Use gender-neutral or plural constructions.
Use he/him or she/her only if those pronouns match the person being described.
Avoid combination forms such as s/he, he/she, or (s)he.
Singular “they” is acceptable when referring to people who use it or when gender is unknown, though acceptance varies by style guide.
Avoid gendered job titles and suffixes.
Use “female” as an adjective in parallel constructions (e.g., “25 male and 48 female students”).
Never use “lady” as an adjective (e.g., “lady scientist”).
Refer to individuals using their self-identified name and pronouns, even in reference to past events.
Use “gay” and “lesbian” instead of “homosexual,” and acknowledge nonbinary identities with inclusive terms like “all genders.”
Note on Singular “They”
Although singular “they” has centuries of usage, its role in formal writing remains debated. AMA and APA accept it in cases of unknown or nonbinary gender, while CMOS permits it under certain conditions. Rewriting to avoid pronouns is often the best alternative.

Racial and Ethnic Identity
To promote equity and accuracy, writers should avoid generalisations and outdated terms. The AMA, APA, and CMOS all offer guidance.

Key Recommendations
Be specific.
Capitalise names of ethnic and national groups.
Follow your style guide regarding capitalising “Black” and “White.”
Avoid “BIPOC” unless explicitly preferred by the group being described.
Indigenous Peoples
Terminology for Indigenous peoples varies across regions and communities. Writers should aim to reflect each group’s preferred terms and identities.

Best Practices
Capitalise “Indigenous” when referring to original inhabitants in Australian and Canadian English.
Refer to people by their self-identified names (e.g., “Inuit,” “First Nations”).
Seek guidance from relevant community organisations when unsure.
Indigenous peoples have a wide range of different identities, cultures and experiences, and the language we use should reflect that.

Disability
Two widely accepted approaches to discussing disability are person-first and identity-first language.

Person-first: Emphasises the individual (e.g., “person with paraplegia”).
Identity-first: Emphasises the identity (e.g., “Deaf person”) and is often used by self-advocates.
Use either approach respectfully, and follow the preference of the community or individual where known.

Terms to Avoid 
Restrictive terms (e.g., “wheelchair-bound”).
Labels suggesting passivity (e.g., “AIDS victim”).
Slurs or condescending euphemisms (e.g., “invalid,” “special needs”).
Use specific and neutral terms (e.g., “person with schizophrenia”).

Age
Avoid stigmatising terms when writing about age.

Suggestions
Use specific data ranges when reporting research.

Avoid terms like “senile” or “geriatric” when referring to people (“geriatric ward” is acceptable).
Avoid euphemisms and generalisations.
Socioeconomic Status
Writers should use clear, respectful language when referring to economic or social class.

Best Practices
Avoid terms like “poor people.”
Use “individuals with low income” or “under-resourced individuals.”
Avoid loaded phrases such as “welfare-reliant” or “poverty-stricken.”
Avoid assumptions about values or capability based on socioeconomic status.
