Implicit Bias
This phenomenon can occur even in those who actively and intentionally resist discriminatory beliefs and actions. Unintentional judgments may provoke negative thoughts and decisions about individuals belonging to groups outside of their own, while evoking positive thoughts within one's own group. Beyond being antithetical to inclusivity, implicit bias impacts clinical decision-making and health care outcomes. Specific to the school setting, implicit racial biases have been identified as a factor in school teachers' disciplinary actions, with bias impacting a choice for punitive discipline over rehabilitative approaches.

It is important to acknowledge that implicit biases are present in everyone, even in well-meaning health care providers. Because implicit bias plays a significant role in influencing how nurses provide care to their patients, school nurses should examine their own implicit biases through self-reflection. Taking an implicit bias test may assist the school nurse in gaining insight into their own unconscious biases. It is hopeful that the results of the IAT will motivate individuals to reflect and make a commitment to try to shift their implicit preferences. School nurses should remember that implicit biases are present in everyone, and it is not meant to shame or punish the test-taker.

Acknowledgment of one's own implicit biases allows for conscious change and can serve as an impetus for working to provide more inclusive care to students. When school nurses are aware of their implicit biases, it is critical to devote time to self-reflection and investigate how personal experiences may influence this bias. Seeking opportunity to get to know individuals with different personal characteristics and lived experiences, purposefully recognizing each person as a unique individual, and engaging in deliberate self-reflection are helpful approaches for mitigating implicit biases. Enrolling in a professional development workshop with a focus on unconscious bias training is an actionable step that can benefit the school nurse's practice, lead to personal growth and transformation, and demonstrate a dedication to recognizing biases. Person-first language has been a long-supported and promoted concept in nursing. Using person-first language aims to promote human dignity through emphasizing the person rather than their condition or illness. For instance, rather than labeling a student as an "asthmatic," the nurse should refer to them as "a student with asthma." Choosing to use person-first terminology that describes the person versus the illness demonstrates a commitment from the nurse that recognizes the student as a person beyond their health challenges.

While person-first language can be supportive, the individual should ultimately decide how they may like to be discussed in the context of their condition. Recently, people with select health conditions have brought attention to the positive aspects or great effect they feel their health condition has on who they are as a whole person. For example, people with autism may choose to be called "autistic" instead of "a person with autism," as they feel that a person-first language organization does not respect neurodiversity or the cultural identity and experience of being autistic. As another example, individuals who are deaf may prefer "a deaf person" over "a person with deafness" as the person-first term may separate the person from an important aspect of themselves. During school enrollment, an intake interview, or a health encounter, school nurses should ask the student and caregiver how they identify with their condition. For example, the school nurse may say, "I want to make sure that I am being respectful. People often refer to their condition in different ways. How do you refer to your condition, or talk about yourself having the condition?" Asking these important questions shows support for the use of the student's chosen way to address their condition and demonstrates the nurse's commitment to inclusivity and support for belonging.

Cultural Humility and Competence

Cultural humility and competence are often used interchangeably in the professional health care setting. However, these terms do not address the same thoughts or actions regarding inclusive practice. Identifying the differences between cultural competence and cultural humility is essential, as cultural humility is the concept that best promotes a culture of inclusivity and belonging.

For instance, when working with a student who has recently arrived in the United States from India, a school nurse might take the initiative to acquaint themselves with the culture, religious beliefs, communication preferences, and dietary limitations of India beforehand. However, this approach has limitations, as school nurses may unintentionally assume they already understand an individual based on their place of origin or what language they speak. Religious, cultural, and dietary inclinations may differ among individuals from India, underscoring the significance for school nurses to establish a personalized understanding of each student.

When incorporating cultural humility into the approach, school nurses understand that it is not realistic to become competent in another's culture. As noted, school nurses should first begin by engaging in self-reflection to identify their own biases. Avoiding assumptions, such as assuming that a student from India abstains from consuming beef or is Hindu, supports an inclusive environment and prepares the nurse to foster belonging for the family. Employing cultural humility demonstrates that the nurse has made a commitment to personal growth and allows them to engage in a more holistic and inclusive practice while working with students.

Although school nurses are committed to bettering the health of students through fostering belonging, it is important to recognize that mistakes may occur despite a commitment to inclusivity. In moments of missteps, others may engage in "calling in" or "calling out" to demonstrate their support for inclusivity and potential for positive change. Calling out may involve conveying shame and confrontation, whereas calling in is usually seen as empathetic and an opportunity for education. Calling out in front of others may be embarrassing for someone, whereas calling in creates a safe environment that is designed for constructive change.

For example, if a school nurse overhears a teacher saying to a student in front of the class at snack time, "Pat, since you have diabetes, be sure to not eat anything with sugar," the school nurse could call in their colleague. In this and similar situations, the nurse may initiate an open and honest conversation with the teacher that is focused on personal growth. A suggested response to the teacher could be, I overheard your conversation with Pat about sugar. Students with diabetes can have sugar; however, it requires careful monitoring. The entire class might not know about Pat's health condition. Let's plan to chat more later on about how to best support Pat.

Pronouns

School nurses should regularly examine their state's guidelines, as certain states have implemented regulations related to the use of pronouns. When caring for students, it is crucial for school nurses to use pronouns that students identify with. Proper pronoun use demonstrates respect, validates the unique identities of every student, and fosters an inclusive environment where students feel like they belong. At times, one may form assumptions or judgments about another's gender based on the person's name or appearance. Such assumptions may be harmful to the individual, as misgendering and a failure to respect pronouns is associated with adverse mental health outcomes.

Asking students and families about their pronouns should be as commonplace as asking for their phone number or address during school intake. School nurses should avoid asking, "What are your preferred pronouns?" Using the word preferred implies that gender identity is a choice. A suggested way for school nurses to ask students and families about pronouns or gender identity is by saying, "I want to be respectful and learn more about you. What is your gender identity and what pronouns do you use?" Another method of talking about pronouns involves the school nurse normalizing sharing pronouns when first meeting a student or family. The school nurse could say, "I'm Marco, the elementary school nurse, my pronouns are he/they," which may prompt the student or family member to share the same. Other ways for school nurses to support pronoun sharing is by adding their pronouns to their email signatures, school name badges, handouts, Zoom account name, and office signage.
