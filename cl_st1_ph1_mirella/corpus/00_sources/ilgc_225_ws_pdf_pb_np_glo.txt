Ethnicity
Race and Ethnicity (someone's ethnic background, their ethnicity, ethnic groups, etc) -
The UK government uses ‘ethnicity’ rather than ‘race.’ Race tends to relate more to
physical characteristics such as skin colour, whereas ethnicity relates more to social and
cultural factors.

Heritage – Similar to ethnicity.

Culture (cultural background, cultural heritage) – often social behaviour that contributes to
identity, such as someone’s language, religion, society, habits, and celebrations.

Ethnicities other than White British - helpful on the occasions where we need to speak
broadly. 'Minority ethnic' and 'ethnic minority,' as well as 'global majority' can be used
similarly. Be aware of when this is being used though, as it groups together a lot of people
with very diverse ethnicities.

Anti-racism – is a more intentional approach to tackling racism and encourages
unlearning some of the ideas and systems engrained in society.

Black, Black person – a person who is of African or African Caribbean heritage. ‘Black’
should be capitalised when used to refer to someone’s identity. In this context it should be
used as an adjective to describe someone (e.g. a Black person, that person is Black) and
not as a noun to refer to a group of people (e.g. the Blacks).

White, White person – a racial classification that refers to skin colour. It should be used in
the same way as ‘Black;’ as an adjective and not a noun.

Biracial, Multiracial – The phrase ‘mixed race’ carries connotations of racial purity and is
no longer used unless someone chooses to identify themselves this way. Biracial or
multiracial refers to someone who has a generational lineage of differing races.

Caste, Casteism –
‘Caste’ refers to the hereditary class system of Hindu society, based
on the idea of ritual purity. This system exists in India and Nepal. ‘Casteism’ refers to
prejudice or discrimination on the basis of caste. It wasn’t until the British colonisation of
India that the caste system became so entrenched, divisive, and so closely tied to
colourism. The British Raj deliberately gave more rights and access to Brahmins in
shaping colonial policy as part of a ‘divide and conquer’ tactic.

Outdated and unacceptable terms when talking about race and ethnicity:
• N-word – an incredibly derogatory word that was used to remove the humanity of
enslaved people. It is sometimes used within Black communities to reclaim the slur
but should not be used outside of that.
• BAME – groups together a whole group of diverse people. If the term is needed, it
should be used in full (Black, Asian, Minority Ethnic) but it’s better to be specific.
• Half-caste, Mulatto – Offensive terms given to multiracial children during
colonisation.
• Eskimo – This is a name given to Inuit people during colonisation.
• Red Indians – This is a name given to Native American or Indigenous people
during colonisation.

Sexuality and Gender
LGBTQ+ – The most commonly used acronym to refer to the Lesbian, Gay, Bisexual,
Transgender, and Queer community in the UK. There are other acronyms but including
‘Q+’ acknowledges the intersectionality and diversity within the community.
Sexuality, sexual orientation – used to describe who someone is attracted to. It often
includes romantic attraction too, and includes asexual identities under its umbrella.
Gender, gender identity – used to describe the way that someone identifies their gender.
This is different to ‘sex’ which is often used to refer to someone’s biology.
Cis, cisgender – A person whose gender identity is the same as the sex they were
assigned at birth. It should be used as an adjective and not a noun (e.g. ‘a cisgender
person’, not ‘a cisgender’).
Trans, transgender – A person whose gender identity is different to the sex they were
assigned at birth. It should be used as an adjective and not a noun (e.g. ‘a trans person’,
not ‘a transgender’ or ‘transgendered’).
Non-binary – Can be used as an umbrella term for people who do not identify within a
gender binary (woman or man). There are lots of identities included in this umbrella. Non-
binary people are included in the umbrella term ‘trans.’
Intersex – A term that a person may use when they are born with sex characteristics that
do not fit the typical binary idea of male or female. These include genitalia, hormones,
chromosomes, and reproductive organs. Being intersex is a variation, not a disease, and is
often not visible.

Outdated and unacceptable terms when talking about gender and sexuality:
• LBG – Often used in transphobic rhetoric to explicitly exclude the transgender
community. The full acronym should be used unless you are only talking about
sexuality, though it’s best to specify in that case.
• Biological male/female - Biology is rarely as binary as we’re led to believe, and
this phrase carries transphobic attitudes. ‘Assigned female/male at birth’
(AFAB/AMAB) are more accurate and should be used if necessary.
• Transsexual (or shortened slang versions of this word) – an older term to refer
to transgender people. Some people still identify with this term, but it should only be
used when someone is comfortable.
• F-word – an offensive and outdated term pre-dating 1914 when it was first written in
‘A Vocabulary of Criminal Slang’

Disability
Non-disabled – A factual and neutral term to refer to someone who doesn’t have a
disability.
Living with/affected by a condition – This phrasing allows a neutral tone rather than one
that implies negativity. Phrases like ‘suffering from,’ ‘a victim of,’ and ‘afflicted with’ should
be avoided.
Person with a mobility/physical condition – To describe a person affected by a
particular disability while keeping the person at the centre. This can apply to other
conditions too.
Deaf person, hard of hearing person, person with a hearing condition – To describe a
person affected by hearing loss. It should be recognised that there are different degrees of
impairment. The word ‘deaf’ can describe anyone with a severe hearing condition, and
‘Deaf’ (capitalised) is sometimes used to refer to people who have been deaf their whole
lives and use sign language as their first language.
Blind person, visually impaired person, person with a visual condition – To describe
a person affected by sight loss. As with deafness, there are degrees to this. ‘Blind person’
refers to someone with total sight loss.
People living with mental health conditions – By using ‘living with’ we can refer to a
person’s mental health condition without stigmatising it, and avoid conflating day-to-day
behaviours with diagnosed illnesses. We try to avoid mental health ‘issues’ and ‘problems’
to similarly remove negative connotations.

Outdated and unacceptable terms when talking about disability and neurodiversity:
• Crippled, wheelchair-bound, invalid, handicapped – These have negative
connotations, and imply restriction which is not necessarily true.
• Phrases that misrepresent mental health issues, such as “I get OCD about
tidying,” “They’re psychotic,” “Their mood swings are bipolar” should not be used as
this undermines and lessens the experiences of people with these conditions.
• Spastic, Spaz – an outdated and offensive term which was used heavily in 1980’s.
• Retarded – outdated and offensive word which was previously used in medicine to
describe intellectual disabilities.
