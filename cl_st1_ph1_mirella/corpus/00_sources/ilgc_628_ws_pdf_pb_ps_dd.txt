AGE


INSTEAD OF: THE ELDERLY
TRY: Mature individual

INSTEAD OF: THE OLD
TRY: Older people

INSTEAD OF: THE YOUNG
TRY: Young/younger people

WHY? These general terms imply that people of certain ages are a standardised group.
At which point is someone old or young? How would you feel if someone defined you as old or young?

INSTEAD OF: BOYS/GIRLS WHEN REFERRING TO ADULTS
TRY: Men/women
TRY: Colleagues

WHY? These words are used to refer to children, consider that your adult colleagues may prefer being called men/women.
Some women may self-identify as girls, but as a group that is often underrepresented in leadership positions, others can find the term demeaning (see further reading for more information).


DISABILITY

INSTEAD OF: THE DISABLED
INSTEAD OF: THE HANDICAPPED
TRY: Person with a disability
TRY: People with disabilities
TRY: Disabled people/person

WHY? Always put the person before the disability, they may not want to be defined by it.
The word handicapped has a number of problematic connotations: the word implies inherent inability, not being able to function, separation from society as a whole (see further reading).
Some people with disabilities may identify with terms like 'disabled'. This is their decision and should be respected.


INSTEAD OF: NORMAL
INSTEAD OF: HEALTHY
INSTEAD OF: ABLE-BODIED PERSON
TRY: People without a disability
TRY: Non-disabled person

WHY? Avoid using terms that imply living with a disability is unhealthy or abnormal.
Disability manifests in many ways including those that have no impact on health or ability.


INSTEAD OF: DISABLED TOILETS
INSTEAD OF: LIFTS FOR THE DISABLED
TRY: Accessible toilets/lifts

WHY? Use positive language rather than descriptions that emphasise limitations.


INSTEAD OF: WHEELCHAIR-BOUND, CONFINED TO A CHAIR
TRY: Person who uses a wheelchair

INSTEAD OF: CANCER OR DEMENTIA VICTIMS
TRY: People living with cancer or dementia

WHY? Avoid negative terms that overextend the severity of a disability.


INSTEAD OF: DEAF AND DUMB
TRY: People who are deaf

INSTEAD OF: NUTS, PSYCHO, MAD
TRY: People with a mental illness

INSTEAD OF: RETARDED, SLOW
TRY: Person with a learning disability

WHY? Avoid outdated and derogatory terms.
Many people use these words in everyday language, not just when describing disability, but there's a growing awareness that these words are hurtful no matter what the context.


DISABILITY CONTINUED

INSTEAD OF: SCHIZOPHRENIC
TRY: Person with schizophrenia

INSTEAD OF: AUTISTIC CHILD
TRY: A child diagnosed with autism

WHY? Avoid terms which equate the person with the ability or disability.


INSTEAD OF: CHALLENGED, SPECIAL
TRY: Person with a learning disability

WHY? Avoid using euphemisms or substitute 'labels'


INSTEAD OF: MENTAL PATIENT, INSANE, MAD, CRAZY
TRY: Person with mental illness

WHY? Mental illness is an illness, and using these terms can perpetuate discrimination.
Bipolar, PTSD, OCD and ADD are real mental health diagnoses that people possess.
Using these terms to describe everyday behaviours can dismiss a person's experiences with mental disorders (see further reading).


GENDER


INSTEAD OF: MAN, MANKIND
TRY: Humans, Humankind

INSTEAD OF: WORKMANSHIP
TRY: Quality of work/skills

INSTEAD OF: MAN THE DESK/PHONES
TRY: Attend/answer the phones

INSTEAD OF: MAN-MADE
TRY: Artificial, manufactured, synthetic

WHY? Use gender neutral terms.
They are more representative of the world we live in.


INSTEAD OF: ANYONE WHO WANTS HIS WORK EVALUATED
TRY: Anyone who wants their work evaluated

INSTEAD OF: WELCOME LADIES AND GENTLEMEN
TRY: Welcome friends and colleagues
TRY: Welcome everyone/folks

WHY? Use gender neutral pronouns and expressions that don't exclude certain groups.


INSTEAD OF: THE TRANSGENDER
TRY: Trans people/Trans person, Trans

INSTEAD OF: IT
TRY: Transgender identity and expression

WHY? Use person-centred language.
Never use 'it'. You are talking about a person, not a thing.


INSTEAD OF: SHE, HER, HERS AND HE, HIM, HIS
TRY: They, them, theirs. It is correct to use in the singular (e.g. Xena ate their food because they were hungry).

WHY? Avoid assuming someone's gender based on their appearance or name, as some people may not identify as a man or a woman, but would instead prefer to choose their pronouns, commonly: he, she, they or ze (see below definition for more information).
You should respect the way people wish to identify themselves. If you aren't sure which pronouns to use, just ask them!
Using 'they' and other gender-neutral language is completely acceptable unless otherwise requested.
This is also a way to show your support for gender non-conforming groups.


SEXUALITY

INSTEAD OF: SEXUAL PREFERENCE
INSTEAD OF: LIFESTYLE CHOICE
TRY: Sexual orientation

WHY? Avoid terms that suggest a degree of voluntary choice when this is not the case.


INSTEAD OF: LESBIANS, GAYS, BISEXUALS
TRY: Lesbian, gay, bisexual, queer people

WHY? Use person-centred language.
Use this language if someone has told you so, you shouldn't assume otherwise. In some instances, a person could be in a same sex relationship but not identify as gay or lesbian.
For example some women would identify with the word gay rather than lesbian.


INSTEAD OF: INVITE YOUR BOYFRIEND/HUSBAND/GIRLFRIEND/WIFE
TRY: Invite your spouse/partner

WHY? Use language that does not assume heterosexuality as the norm.


INSTEAD OF: MOTHER AND FATHER
TRY: Parents, caregivers

WHY? Recognise diverse family formations.


RACE, ETHNICITY, NATIONALITY


INSTEAD OF: ASIANS
TRY: Asian people

WHY? Use adjectives rather than nouns when it is necessary to refer to someone's ethnicity.


INSTEAD OF: "CHINESE PEOPLE ARE REALLY GOOD AT MATHS"
TRY: Just don't say it

WHY? Avoid stereotyping, e.g., making positive or negative generalisations about members of a particular racial, ethnic or national group.


INSTEAD OF: COLOURED
TRY: …Asking yourself why you need to refer to someone's ethnicity in the first place – Is it necessary to the discussion?
TRY: People of colour
TRY: Biracial or mixed race
TRY: If you are talking about a specific ethnicity, you should say so. For example, black people.

WHY? Use this language if someone has told you so, you shouldn't assume otherwise.
In the UK the term coloured is, at best, seen as old fashioned. But it's also regarded as a highly offensive racial slur which recalls a time when casual racism was a part of everyday life. In the US it is among the most offensive words for describing a black person.
People of colour is an accepted term when talking about ethnic minorities generally, but it shouldn't be used when you're talking about a specific ethnicity.
BAME is an outdated and problematic term, namely that it treats ethnic minorities as being in the same non-white group, without acknowledging individual issues.


INSTEAD OF: MINORITY GROUPS
TRY: Underrepresented backgrounds, underrepresented groups, marginalised identity groups or specify a specific ethnic group. E.g. The Asian community

WHY? 'Minority' is sometimes used as a blanket term for people from underrepresented groups, but underrepresented doesn't always equal a minority. For instance, black, Asian and other visible minority ethnic groups in the UK are actually a global majority.
