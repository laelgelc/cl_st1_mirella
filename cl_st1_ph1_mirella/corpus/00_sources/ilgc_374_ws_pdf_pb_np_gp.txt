KEY PRINCIPLES FOR INCLUSIVE LANGUAGE

Consider Perspective Hive's principles of inclusive language across all forms of communication, whether you are speaking with colleagues and clients, writing emails and other documents, drafting job descriptions, or creating various forms of content.

- Is it necessary? Do we need to qualify a person's individual characteristics?
- Does it amplify a stereotype? Such as overemphasising certain characteristics of people.
- Is it disrespectful to, or does it exclude certain groups of people? Some terms and phrases may not resonate with or alienate groups of people whose experiences and identities are different.
- Is it deficit-based? Focusing on what people can do, rather than what they cannot, builds a more inclusive culture that values the unique attributes everyone brings.

WHAT TO DO IF YOU GET IT WRONG

Embracing an inclusive language approach requires a commitment to continuous learning and self-improvement, acknowledging that mistakes are part of the journey and actively seeking to understand and rectify them. If you get it wrong:

- Recognise that mistakes happen and commit to learning from them.
- Understand that diversity, equity, and inclusion are evolving concepts. Stay open to learning and refining your practices to ensure ongoing inclusivity.
- If you realise you've used the wrong terminology, apologise immediately and correct yourself. If necessary, clarify the correct term, and then move on without further fuss.
- If you're unsure about a topic or experience a negative interaction, discuss it with a trusted colleague or someone knowledgeable on the subject for guidance.
- Take time to reflect on the mistake, understand its impact, and educate yourself on why it was inappropriate. Use resources such as training materials, articles, or discussions to deepen your understanding and prevent similar errors in the future.

It is a mark of respect to be as specific about someone's cultural identity as possible. Using the name that describes a person's identity and cultural or language group (e.g., Gamilaroi, Tharawal, Bundjalung, Pitjantjatjara) is preferable for some people – for example "Deborah is a Wurundjeri Elder".

If you are not aware of the way a person describes themselves, ask the person or consult with local Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples. Be aware though that some Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people may relate to their culture but not know their specific language or cultural group due to the impact of colonisation, displacement, and historic policies of removal.

In some parts of Australia, the term 'Indigenous' can be considered offensive. This is due to connotations derived from historical documents that described Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples as part of the 'flora/fauna' rather than the human population of Australia. It can be seen as a problematic universalising or homogenising label for what are, in reality, highly diverse identities. Reconciliation Australia advises that an exception for the term 'Indigenous' is considered in some situations, such as when an Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander person or organisation prefers it, when it is part of a program or job title, or when it is embedded in organisational policies like those of Federal or State Governments or the United Nations.

STEREOTYPES TO AVOID

Ensure that the following terms are avoided when describing Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples as they can perpetuate negative stereotypes.

- Disadvantaged 'deficit language' – instead it is important to use empowering, strengths-based language. Be careful not to perpetuate patronising or paternalistic rhetoric. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples and cultures have survived across the Australian continent for tens of thousands of years and, as such, are not 'in need' of being 'rescued' or 'saved'.

- Under no circumstances should generalisations or stereotypes associated with skin colour or other physical characteristics be made with reference to First Nations peoples. Assimilationist terms such as 'full-blood', 'half-caste' and 'quarter-caste' are extremely offensive and should never be used when referring to Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples. Neither is it appropriate to ask 'what percentage Aboriginal' a person is.

- Avoid using possessive language such as 'our' when referring to Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples as it risks implying ownership over people and does not recognise the self-governance and self-determination of these individuals and communities.

It is important that inclusive workplaces in Aotearoa recognise the special status of te reo Maori and use it correctly, respectfully, and frequently to contribute to its protection and strong growth.

Ensure that the following assumptions and word usage is avoided when referring to Maori as they can perpetuate stereotypes.

- Assuming that all Maori can speak te reo Maori. Following colonisation, the use of te reo Maori was frequently discouraged and even punished. Urbanisation and intermarriage further separated many Maori from te reo Maori-speaking environments. This had a devastating effect over generations and many Maori did not grow up hearing and speaking their language. Thanks to Maori language champions, te reo Maori is making a strong comeback in Aotearoa, however there are still obstacles to some Maori to learning their language, including access to learning opportunities and overcoming stigma.
- Assuming that te reo Maori experts in your own organisation are comfortable and have time to give you advice. Consider how much you ask them to advise you on top of their everyday job responsibilities. Recognise that you may need to pay for external expertise in te reo Maori, as you do for other expertise.
- Depending on context, don't assume that kupu Maori (Maori words) are referring to Maori only e.g. 'rangatahi' can mean young adult of any heritage, whereas 'rangatahi Maori' specifically means young Maori adult.
- Using the word 'our' to refer to indigenous peoples can be problematic. Rather than communicating inclusively, it can have possessive and patronising connotations. It's a good idea to consider the context and whether there are other alternate options to reflect your intent.


Generally, if someone's age is not relevant – and it is unlikely to be relevant in most work situations – then don't mention it. If it is relevant, then reference it respectfully and be explicit when possible (for example, say "adults over 50" or "adults over 60," rather than terms like "seniors" or "the elderly"). Mentioning someone's age unnecessarily can create the idea that the person referred to is somehow an oddity and reinforce notions that they are a 'special case'.

Take caution when referring to groups or individuals using retirement-centred terms such as 'retired people' or 'retirees'. For many, this is a celebrated life stage and thus they may self-identify with terms like 'retiree'. For others, such terms might be seen as reductionist or misrepresenting their ongoing contribution either via paid or unpaid work.

The language we use plays a critical role in either perpetuating or preventing stereotypes. Avoid using the following terms as they can reinforce negative age-based stereotypes.

- Catch-all, pop-culture terms like 'boomer' or 'millennial' can be over-homogenising and perceived as intergenerational finger-pointing. They do not reflect the full range of lived experiences because people in the same age group hold diverse identities, views, experiences, and interests. As such, avoid generational terms unless referring to a specific generational study.
- Consider also how visuals might perpetuate age-based stereotypes and assumptions. For example, after analysing over 1,000 images from brands and media, the American Association of Retired Persons (AARP) found that older adults were often excluded entirely. Despite making up almost half of the U.S. population, they were shown in only 15% of photos. To help combat age-bias in imagery, AARP teamed up with Getty Images to introduce a more realistic collection of images showing older people running businesses, playing basketball and hanging out with younger generations.
- Be wary of ageist assumptions, some common examples:
  - Assuming older people aren't suited to technology roles and attributing tech-savviness to younger people.
  - Attributing disinterest in career progression to older individuals or categorising younger professionals as 'job hoppers'.
  - Assuming that coping with change is more challenging for older people or perceiving younger individuals as requiring more 'handholding'.
  - Associating entitlement or laziness with younger generations.
  - Assuming older people are a homogenous group; for instance, 'people 65 and over' is a group of unique people with different ages spanning over a number of years. The only distinctive quality at age 65 and over is eligibility for national superannuation.
- Avoid patronising language, even if the goal is to compliment or praise. For instance, don't talk about someone looking "good for their age" or being "X years young".

CA ANZ follows best-practice inclusive language by using the term 'person/people with a disability' or 'person/people with disabilities'. This grammatical expression covers both the singular and plural ways of referencing either one or more impairments or conditions and puts the person rather than the disability first.

However, we recognise individual preferences and that some people may choose to be identified by their disability first. If you are unsure how someone prefers to be identified, it's always best to ask.

Avoid the following word usage as it can perpetuate negative accessibility-based stereotypes.

- Assuming people with disability are victims and/or helpless.
- Portraying people with disability as inspirational, for example saying "You're so brave/inspirational/courageous" can be patronising and reductive.
- Assuming incompetence by believing that people with disability are incapable of doing what others can.
- Treating accessibility as 'special treatment' by viewing adjustments as a favour rather than a basic right necessary for equitable participation.


Some people on the autism spectrum identify as autistic/Autistic. They consider autism to be a cultural identity beyond the medical diagnosis and may or may not identify as having disability. It is appropriate to describe someone who identifies this way as 'an autistic person', but do not call anyone 'an autistic' (similar to 'the disabled').

The language used to refer to autism differs between Australia and New Zealand. The autistic community largely prefers identity first as the default for autistic people. The preferences of individual autistic people should always be respected. If an autistic person would like to be referred to using language that differs from most autistic people, use the individual's preference.

GENDER, SEX AND SEXUALITY


When referring to gender, sex or sexuality, it is important that these terms are not conflated. While they are interconnected aspects of identity, it is critical to acknowledge the diverse experiences and expressions within them.

'Gender identity' is a person's gender
Gender is a social and cultural concept about the identity, expression and experience as a person of a particular gender (e.g. man, woman, non-binary person, genderfluid person, gender diverse person etc).

'Sex' is based on biological sex characteristics
A person's sex is based upon their biological sex characteristics, such as their chromosomes, hormones and reproductive organs. While typically based upon the sex characteristics observed and recorded at birth or infancy, a person's sex can change over the course of their lifetime and may differ from their sex recorded at birth.

'Sexuality' is who a person is attracted to
Sexuality relates to the sexual orientation of a person which encapsulates sexual identity (how a person thinks of their sexuality and the terms they identify with), attraction (romantic or sexual interest in another person) and behaviour.


Avoid the following word usage as it can perpetuate negative gender-based stereotypes.

- Always be mindful of gender stereotypes. Deloitte found that more flexible ideas around gender could lead to an additional $128 billion each year for Australia's economy – or an opportunity cost of equal amount. Challenging traditional gender norms unlocks a larger talent pool, sparks innovation, and boosts productivity in businesses. It's important to think carefully before characterising women as carers or men as authoritative. Closely consider potential bias in the use of images, labels, characterisations and clichés.
- The interchangeable use of gender and sex. Talk about 'women entrepreneurs' or 'men taking parental leave', rather than referring to 'males' and 'females' in circumstances not specifically relevant to individuals' sex characteristics.
- Dismissing or questioning the legitimacy of someone's gender identity, insisting it's just a phase or denying its validity entirely e.g. continuing to use their former name rather than the name they have stated they prefer and identify with if they have transitioned their gender.
- Perceiving or portraying people who are transgender as deceptive, opportunistic or attention-seeking and focusing excessively on their physical transition or anatomical characteristics rather than their identity and experiences.
- Assuming that gender is binary, leading to the belief that there are only two pronoun options: he/him for men and she/her for women. This overlooks non-binary and gender-nonconforming individuals who may prefer pronouns such as they/them or other pronouns.

'Race' is often used to group people based on shared physical traits, particularly skin colour and hair texture, and a shared ancestry or historical experience as a result.

'Ethnicity' and/or 'cultural background' are often self-selected and associated with cultural heritage or ethnic group. They refer to shared cultural or national identities or ancestry, encompassing language, nationality, religious practices, and/or customs.

It is important to note that there is no genetic or biological basis for either race, cultural background or ethnicity; they are socially constructed concepts. All human groups belong to the species Homosapiens and share 99.9% of genetic material.

Also note that these are complex and multi-dimensional concepts. Many individuals identify with multiple cultural or ethnic groupings, like being both Australian and Italian or New Zealander and Chinese. Self-identification is important and wherever possible, allow people to nominate how they would like their ethnic identity referred to, for example, by cultural group such as 'Asian Australian' or by specific country of origin, for example, "Chinese Australian".

Cultural stereotyping is common in the workplace. It's critical to recognise the impact of words and phrases that can perpetuate harmful generalisations.

- Associating accents and language proficiency with intelligence is inappropriate. Comments like 'you speak English so well for a [nationality/ethnicity]' or 'you speak very good English for a foreigner' may be intended as compliments but come across as condescending, exclusionary behaviours instead. These remarks perpetuate the stereotype that speakers with English as a second language are generally less capable.
- Anglicising names from diverse cultural backgrounds by altering them to make them sound more 'Western' or using phrases like 'that's a hard name to pronounce' is disrespectful and dismissive of a person's cultural identity. The onus is on everyone to do their work and to value the spelling and pronunciation of colleagues' and clients' names. If in doubt, ask them about their preference and pronunciation.
- Categorising all individuals of a race or ethnicity into a single cultural or behavioural stereotype by using phrases like 'all Asians are good at math' or 'all Pacific Island people excel in rugby' ignores the diversity and individuality within these groups.

Our language aims to acknowledge and celebrate the diverse family structures and caregiving arrangements in society, whether discussing pregnancy, parenting, or caregiving dynamics.


Although traditional gender roles in parenting have shifted, deep-rooted assumptions and stereotypes remain. It's important to be sensitive and inclusive when discussing pregnancy, parenting and caregiving. Below are some stereotypes to avoid.

- Assuming that new parents are uninterested in career advancement including important project roles or development opportunities.
- Assuming there is a typical or "normal" experience of pregnancy and parenting overlooks the wide variability in individual experiences and circumstances. This stereotype fails to acknowledge the diverse range of challenges and joys that parents may encounter.
- Undermining the diverse range of parenting styles and capabilities among individuals of all genders. For example, perpetuating the stereotype that women inherently possess a natural maternal instinct that makes them superior caregivers undermines the role of men or those that identify as non-binary parents, as well as other non-maternal caregivers such as grandparents.
- Assuming families adhere to traditional norms instead of recognising that some individuals may have experienced IVF, adoption, surrogacy, or donor conception, or may be guardians or carers of children.
- Assuming that everyone was raised by a traditional two- or single parent family structure. Some individuals were raised by siblings, grandparents, adoptive parents, other relatives and in some instances, friends. Mother's Day and Father's Day holds varied significance for each person, so be sensitive to this.

RACE AND ETHNICITY

Australia and New Zealand are vibrant multicultural societies, shaped by the diverse cultural identities that form our workplaces and communities. Individuals from diverse backgrounds enrich the social fabric, with multiculturalism a driving force for economic success (inc. productivity, talent, trade, and tourism) and social cohesion (inc. community resilience, innovation and cultural exchange) on both sides of the Tasman. Recognising and embracing this diversity not only fosters inclusivity, understanding and mutual respect, it maximises the many rich and varied economic, social and other benefits.


PREGNANCY, PARENTING AND CARING

CA ANZ advocates for inclusive language that speaks to real experiences, rather than perpetuating outdated gender norms. Shared parental leave over and above statutory requirements is now available in many organisations, with 60% of employers offering access to paid parental leave according to the Australian Workplace Gender Equality Agency. Using terms like 'parents' instead of gender-specific titles acknowledges the diverse roles individuals play in raising children. This includes references to parental leave rather than outdated terms like maternity leave and paternity leave.
