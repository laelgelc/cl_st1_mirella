WHAT IS INCLUSIVE LANGUAGE?

Inclusive language is communication that proactively uses words, phrases and expressions that welcome and include, and avoids assumptions that may stereotype or exclude.

Non-inclusive language is most often unintentional, but it can have a negative influence on the way we work and connect with our colleagues. For example, use of a gender-specific term such as 'Chairman of the Board' is non-inclusive, when the inclusive alternative could simply be 'Chair of the Board'.

"Language is a powerful tool for inclusion as it helps us to more accurately represent the diverse society we live in. When we use inclusive language, we signal our commitment to respecting and valuing each individual's unique identity, experiences and perspectives, while fostering effective collaboration and communication."
— Ainslie van Onselen, CA ANZ CEO

WHY INCLUSIVE LANGUAGE IS IMPORTANT

Language often reflects both conscious and unconscious biases. By being mindful of our language, we can address and reduce these biases. Confronting non-inclusive language can be difficult but boosts confidence and workplace inclusion. Inclusive workplace cultures, which strengthen performance and productivity, are built through respectful communication.

"The way we speak to each other creates a culture in which everyone can feel valued, respected, and one of the team (included), rather than under-valued, disrespected, and out of place (excluded)."
— Diversity Council Australia, #WordsAtWork: Building Inclusion through the Power of Language

By adopting language that is considerate and free from bias, organisations proactively comply with legal standards that prohibit discrimination based on race, gender, disability, and other protected characteristics.

We should be most alert to bias in our language when we are under high cognitive load or time pressure. This is when we are most at-risk of falling back on expressions or phrases we have habitually acquired. Unintended bias in our language often occurs when we use:
- Colloquial phrases such as: "it's like the blind leading the blind".
- Describing or generalising people, for example, describing someone as 'just' working part-time.

It can be difficult to understand the lived experiences of others and why they may feel excluded by particular words and phrases. If we are yet to experience feeling excluded in that way, we may find it hard to recognise when we are using biased, stereotypical or exclusionary language. This guide will help.

"Making a conscious decision to avoid particular words and phrases is not about coddling people or shielding them from offense – it's about chipping away at the idea that alienating people through language is acceptable in the first place."
— Jarune Uwujaren, Author
